Variables and data types

A variable is a characteristic, number or quantity that can be measured or counted. Categorical variables describe groups; numerical variables describe quantities.

TypeExample
NominalEye colour
OrdinalSatisfaction rating
DiscreteNumber of pets
ContinuousHeight (cm)

Worked example

Classify number of siblings.

It is a count.
Numerical, discrete.

Now you try

Classify time taken to run 100 m.

Show full solution
It is measured on a continuous scale.
Numerical, continuous.

Use separated bars for categories or discrete values. Include a title, source, axis labels and a consistent scale starting at zero.

Each dot represents one observation. Stack dots above their numerical value.

Pictograms

Use equal-sized symbols and a key. A half symbol represents half the key value.

Now you try

Sector graphs

A sector graph represents parts of a whole. Sector angle = proportion × 360°.

Worked example

Find the angle for B (40%).

40% = 0.4
0.4 × 360° = 144°

Now you try

Find the angle for C (30%).

Show full solution
0.3 × 360° = 108°

Divided bar graphs

A divided bar represents 100% of the observations. Segment lengths are proportional to frequencies.

Worked example

Find the length for B on a 20 cm bar.

40% = 0.4
0.4 × 20 = 8 cm

Now you try

Find the length for A.

Show full solution
0.2 × 20 = 4 cm

Frequency histograms

Group numerical measurements into non-overlapping intervals. For equal-width classes, bar heights show frequency; adjacent bars touch.

Worked example

How many travel times are from 10 to less than 20 minutes?

Read the second bar.
Frequency = 4

Now you try

How many times are below 20 minutes?

Show full solution
2 + 4 = 6

Frequency polygons

Plot each class midpoint against its frequency and join consecutive points with straight lines.

Worked example

Find the midpoint of 10 to less than 20.

(10 + 20) ÷ 2 = 15 minutes

Now you try

Find the frequency at midpoint 25.

Show full solution
Frequency = 3

Line graphs and predictions

Use a line graph for values ordered in time. Describe changes; predictions are estimates, not guarantees.

Worked example

Find the change from Monday to Tuesday.

4 − 2 = 2 books increase

Now you try

Find the change from Tuesday to Thursday.

Show full solution
1 − 4 = −3
Decrease of 3 books

Choosing graphs and misleading displays

Choose a display to suit the variable and purpose. Infographics combine concise text and suitable graphs; include the source. Truncated bar axes can exaggerate differences.

PurposeDisplay
Change over timeLine graph
Parts of a wholeSector or divided bar
Grouped measurementsHistogram
Compare categoriesColumn graph

Worked example

A graph starts at 75 and shows bars for 80 and 90. Why might it mislead?

Actual difference = 90 − 80 = 10.
Visible bar heights are 5 and 15.
The difference appears exaggerated.

Now you try

Choose a graph for monthly rainfall and justify.

Show full solution
A line graph shows change over time. A column graph can also compare monthly totals.
