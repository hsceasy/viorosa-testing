Algebraic techniques

Key ideas

Indices are powers. For the same base, add powers when multiplying and subtract when dividing. A negative power means a reciprocal; a fractional power means a root.

Expand brackets by multiplying every term. Factorising reverses expansion.

For algebraic fractions, factor first, cancel common factors only, and exclude values that made the original denominator zero.

A surd is an irrational root written exactly. To rationalise a denominator containing two terms, multiply numerator and denominator by its conjugate.

A quadratic can be solved by factorising, completing the square or using the quadratic formula. The discriminant b² − 4ac tells us whether there are two, one or no real roots. For rational coefficients, real roots are rational exactly when the discriminant has a rational square root.

Solving a quadratic

  1. Rearrange to ax² + bx + c = 0.
  2. Try factorising. If that is difficult, use completing the square or the quadratic formula.
  3. Solve for x and check the answers.

Worked example — completing the square

Solve x² − 6x + 5 = 0.

Solution

\[x^2-6x=-5\]

\[x^2-6x+9=4\]

\[(x-3)^2=4\]

\[x-3=\pm2\]

\[x=1\text{ or }5\]

Graph transformations

Rewriting a quadratic in completed-square form reveals its shifts. For example, y = (x − 3)² − 4 is y = x² shifted right 3 and down 4.

Adding a number outside the function moves the graph up; subtracting moves it down.

Replacing x with x − h moves the graph right h units; replacing x with x + h moves it left h units (h positive).

Multiplying the output by 2 doubles its height from the x-axis; multiplying by ½ halves it. A negative multiplier also reflects it in the x-axis.

Replacing x by 2x compresses horizontally by factor ½; replacing x by x/2 stretches horizontally by factor 2. Replacing x by −x reflects in the y-axis.
