Weighted networks and shortest paths

Viorosa Maths · Year 11 Mathematics Standard

Key idea

Add edge weights along a route and compare totals, not the number of edges.

Worked example 1

AB = 4, BC = 3, AC = 9: A–B–C costs 7, shorter than AC.

Worked example 2

AB = 2, BD = 8, AC = 4, CD = 3: A–C–D costs 7 versus 10.

Common mistake: Choosing the fewest edges automatically.

Worked example: delivery routes

Weights are travel times in minutes. Find the shortest route from A to F. Compare total weights, not the number of roads.

A–B–D–F: 4 + 5 + 6 = 15. A–C–E–F: 2 + 7 + 3 = 12. A–C–B–D–E–F: 2 + 1 + 5 + 2 + 3 = 13. A–C–B–D–F: 2 + 1 + 5 + 6 = 14.

To verify that no unlisted route is shorter, use Dijkstra’s algorithm: the final distances from A to B, C, D, E and F are 3, 2, 8, 9 and 12 respectively.

Road closure

If C–E closes, the best route becomes A–C–B–D–E–F, taking 13 minutes. The delay is 1 minute.

Try it: if E–F closes instead, what is the shortest route?

A–C–B–D–F, taking 14 minutes.

In another context, the fastest route might cost more in tolls. Always check what the weights measure.
